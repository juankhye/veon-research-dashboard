Telecom 101 · assume you know nothing

How a mobile operator makes money, and why "digital operator" changes the math

Every mobile operator is the same simple machine: customers × price per customer = revenue. The interesting parts are what it costs to run the network, how much must be reinvested each year, and what else you can sell to someone whose identity and payment relationship you already own.

The revenue equation

Revenue = Subscribers × ARPU. ARPU is "average revenue per user" per month. VEON's blended telecom ARPU is about $2 a month; in Kazakhstan it is ~$5, in Bangladesh ~$1. That is why 151m customers only produce ~$3.6bn of telecom revenue a year, whereas a US operator with the same customer count would produce ~$90bn.

Why ARPU is low and why it can still grow

These are poor countries with cheap prepaid SIMs. ARPU grows when (1) users move from 2G/3G voice to 4G data (77% of Jazz's base is now 4G), (2) they consume more gigabytes (8.2 GB/month in Pakistan), (3) prices are raised in line with inflation, and (4) they buy bundles that include digital services. VEON explicitly prices to track nominal GDP: real growth plus inflation. Over 3 to 7 years inflation and currency devaluation roughly cancel out, so local price rises should protect the dollar value of revenue.

The cost stack

  • Cost of services (~12% of revenue): interconnect fees paid to other networks, handset costs in bundles, content licences (cricket rights for Tamasha), payment-scheme fees.
  • Operating costs (~43%): network running costs (energy to power towers is the biggest swing item), tower rent paid to the tower companies VEON sold its towers to, staff, marketing, IT, and sector taxes. Mobile-specific taxes reach 37% of service revenue in Pakistan and 47% in Bangladesh.
  • EBITDA (~44–46%): what is left before depreciation and interest. Telecoms report this because the network is bought upfront and depreciated over years.
  • Capex (15–20% of revenue): new radios, fibre, spectrum licences, batteries and solar. This is cash out the door every year. EBITDA minus capex is the closest thing to true operating cash generation.
  • Below the line: depreciation (~18%), interest on ~$3.5bn of bonds and loans (~$634m a year), a 33% tax rate, and a slice for minority shareholders (mainly the 16.4% of Kyivstar VEON does not own).

Management's punchline

Telecom: 45% margin, 20% capex → 25% of revenue becomes cash. Digital: 36% margin, 7% capex → 29%. So although digital has a lower headline EBITDA margin (and mechanically dilutes the group margin by ~0.1pp for each 1pp of mix shift), it generates more cash per dollar of revenue. That is the whole thesis in two numbers.

What is "spectrum"?

Radio frequencies licensed from the government, usually for 15 years, that a network needs to carry data. More spectrum = more capacity per tower. In March 2026 Jazz bought 190 MHz for ~$240m, the largest allocation in Pakistan's auction, because management says Pakistan is capacity constrained, not demand constrained: build it and usage follows.

What is a "tower sale"?

The steel mast and the land are passive, commodity assets. Selling them to a specialist tower company and renting space back frees cash and moves the maintenance burden. VEON sold 10,500 Pakistan towers for $563m (June 2025) and 2,000 Bangladesh towers for ~$99m. Rent then shows up as a lease liability, which is why VEON quotes leverage both with and without leases.

What is a "digital operator"?

A telecom that treats the SIM as a login and a payment method, then sells non-telecom services through it. VEON's brand for this is DO1440: engage the customer across all 1,440 minutes of the day. The network is not a legacy asset to run down; it is the cheapest customer-acquisition engine in the country, because a standalone fintech or streaming app would have to pay to find the same customers.

Connectivity is the distribution moat. Digital is the margin and the growth.

Revenue and cost drivers · what actually moves the numbers

Six revenue levers, six cost levers, two translation effects

Revenue drivers (ranked by impact)

  • 1. Local-currency price increases tracking nominal GDP. Weighted inflation across markets is ~9.5%; VEON reprices to match. This is the largest single driver of the 17% growth. Telecom ARPU rose 17% YoY in 2Q26.
  • 2. Digital vertical growth. $342m growing 54%: financial services (loans per day, GTV, merchant fees, interest), entertainment (subscriptions, ads), ride-hailing (take rate), healthcare GMV. Contributed ~11 of the 17 points of group growth.
  • 3. Data monetisation. 4G migration (77% in Pakistan), GB per user (8.2), multiplay bundles (45.3m customers at ~4x ARPU).
  • 4. Subscriber growth. Modest: +2–3% in Pakistan and Kazakhstan; declines in Ukraine (population), Bangladesh (competition) and Uzbekistan. ZeroOne models +1.9% group.
  • 5. M&A consolidation. Uklon and Tabletki added ~$41m of 2Q26 revenue; OLX next.
  • 6. Enterprise and adjacent. Identity services, cloud, adtech, sovereign AI, satellite distribution: small today, optional upside.

Cost drivers (ranked by volatility)

  • 1. Energy. Diesel and grid power for tens of thousands of sites. Oil at $90–120 in the Hormuz crisis adds 2–9% inflation to non-producing markets; outages cut usage. Response: 180 MW solar in Ukraine, site-level solar in South Asia.
  • 2. Taxes and regulation. Kazakhstan VAT 12→16% (not passed through); Bangladesh SIM tax and duty rises; 37%/47% sector tax burdens; BTRC claim. These hit margin instantly and reverse only via pricing.
  • 3. Personnel, IT and marketing inflation. Wages track the same 9.5% inflation; digital launches need marketing; content rights (cricket) are dollar-priced.
  • 4. Tower leases. After the tower sales, rent replaces maintenance: ~$1.86bn of lease liabilities. Lower EBITDA volatility, but a fixed cash outflow below EBITDA.
  • 5. Credit provisioning. As JazzCash lends more, expected-loss charges become a real cost line inside financial-services margin (which still expanded to 32.3%).
  • 6. Interest and D&A. ~$634m net interest modelled; new bonds add ~$40m from 2027. D&A ~$900m, rising with spectrum.

Two translation effects to keep separate

FX translation

Revenue is earned in PKR, UAH, KZT, BDT and UZS and reported in USD. A 4% blended devaluation was assumed for 2026 guidance; it is running ~3pp better. Kazakhstan grew 12% in USD but only 4% in tenge in 2Q26 because the currency strengthened.

One-offs in the comparison base

FX and macro · the single largest execution risk

Five soft currencies, one hard reporting currency

A former VEON director called currency and inflation volatility "the single largest risk" to the strategy. The mechanism is simple: costs (wages, energy, dollar-priced equipment and content, dollar bond coupons) inflate immediately, while prices can only be raised in steps. If a currency devalues faster than domestic inflation, dollar margins compress and cash is harder to move to Dubai.

Hyperinflation accounting

In markets deemed hyperinflationary, IAS 29 restates non-monetary items for purchasing power before translating to dollars. It creates non-cash noise in equity and comparability, which is one more reason brokers value the parts rather than the consolidated earnings line.

Cash upstreaming: from opco to Dubai

The bonds are at the Dubai holding company (and a MidCo); the cash is earned in the operating companies. Getting it up requires dividends, management fees or intercompany loans, subject to local capital controls, withholding taxes and, in Ukraine, wartime restrictions.

Ukraine · war

Infrastructure is a target for physical and cyber attack; capex intensity above 25% for three years; grid outages force batteries, solar and Starlink. The bull framing: a settlement would cut the country risk premium and unlock reconstruction demand, and KYIV is the liquid way to play that. Nationalisation risk persists because of the historical UBO link to Russia, even though Ukraine has strong incentives to protect minority investors.

Pakistan · macro and politics

IMF-programme economy; PKR halved against the dollar 2021–23; fragile grid; sector taxation at 37% of service revenue; a well-funded Chinese-owned competitor. Offsetting: the largest, fastest-growing digital ecosystem in the group and a government keen on financial inclusion (Raast payment rails).

Ownership · the LetterOne overhang

LetterOne held ~56% before the Russia exit and 45.46% at end-2025, transferred in Dec 2025 to L1 Shareholding via internal reorganisation. Its ultimate beneficial owners (Fridman, Aven, Kuzmichev, Khan) were sanctioned in 2022; they hold no board seats, no voting rights and no economic benefit under the restructuring, but politically exposed persons still own ~21% indirectly. Some ESG-constrained institutions cannot own the stock, which thins liquidity (avg ~$6.7m a day traded).

Competitive position and moat

Market shares, competitors, and what is actually defensible

Mobile market share by country (%)
VEON is #1 or co-#1 in four of five markets. Bangladesh is the exception.
Source: Northland, Auerbach Grayson, Edison via AlphaSense. Pakistan share estimated from 75.4m Jazz subscribers against a ~190m national base; competitors Zong (China Mobile), Telenor Pakistan (merging with Ufone/PTCL). Uzbekistan: four operators at 21–33% each.

Sources of moat

  • Distribution at near-zero acquisition cost. Standalone fintechs pay to find customers; VEON already bills them.
  • Payment rails and licences. Once a wallet like JazzCash has scale and a licence, it is an oligopoly with high barriers; banks cannot match telecom distribution in unbanked markets.
  • Identity and data. KYC'd SIM base plus payment history is a credit-underwriting asset competitors lack.
  • Switching costs. Multiplay customers churn less; leaving means leaving wallet, TV and loan history.
  • Lighter regulation in frontier markets leaves room to launch services a European operator could not.

Threats to the moat

  • State-backed operators (Kazakhtelecom) with regulatory proximity and subsidies.
  • Deep-pocketed foreign entrants (China Mobile in Pakistan) starting price wars.
  • Specialist digital natives with better product in any single vertical.
  • Handset affordability and low smartphone penetration, especially among women, capping digital adoption.

Bull vs bear

The debate, both sides argued properly

Bull case
  • Digital is real and more profitable than planned. $342m/qtr, +54%, 36% margin vs a 20–25% plan, 7% capex. At 29% cash margin it out-earns telecom per dollar of revenue.
  • JazzCash alone could be worth more than half of VEON ex-Kyivstar. ~$500m revenue, ~$150m EBITDA, growing 50%; fintech peers list at 7–7.5x revenue; Airtel Money's IPO sets a benchmark.
  • The listed Kyivstar stake is ~55% of the market cap, implying the other four countries plus all digital trade at under 2x EBITDA.
  • Guidance raised twice on organic strength; five straight quarters of sequential growth; beats against every broker model.
  • Balance sheet fixed. 1.1x leverage, 2027 wall refinanced, $2.2bn cash, $1bn+ of FCFF dropping to equity from 2027 (Auerbach).
  • Permanent buyback with cancellation (≥$100m/yr, ~2% of market cap) compounds even without a re-rating; dividend optionality FY27–28.
  • Optionality not in any target: Ukraine ceasefire, JazzCash IPO or strategic stake, Bangladesh fintech, sovereign AI, Starlink distribution, OLX.
  • Discount already narrowing: holdco discounts were 30% pre-Russia-exit; brokers now use 10–15%.
Bear case
  • Currency is the killer variable. PKR halved 2021–23; KZT swung 20% in 2025. Costs inflate faster than prices can be raised. Digital assets are early-stage and equally exposed.
  • Ukraine is a war zone and a nationalisation risk because of the historical Russian UBO link; capex intensity above 25% for three years; population shrinking.
  • Kazakhstan shows how fast margins can break: −10pp in a year from a VAT change and bundling accounting, with no pricing response yet.
  • Sub-scale in two markets: #3 in Bangladesh behind a 60%-margin incumbent; 21% share in fragmented Uzbekistan; state-backed rivals with regulatory proximity.
  • Energy fragility: Hormuz-driven fuel shortages cut Bangladesh data usage 15%; grids in Pakistan and Bangladesh cannot support centralised backup.
  • Credit risk is being added to a telecom multiple. 224k loans a day in Pakistan has never been through a credit cycle.
  • Digital growth must normalise and includes acquisitions; group margin mechanically dilutes as mix shifts; 50% target needs M&A that could stretch management.
  • Structural discount may never close: complexity, PEP ownership, thin liquidity (~$7m/day), and a mix of sponsored research. A better business does not guarantee a higher multiple.
